Someone will answer for your legal work in 2040. This guide asks who

Picture the most ordinary task your legal team touches, a supplier renewal, and hold it in mind as this guide moves to 2040. In one future your company built the system that clears it. In another, your general counsel conducts a network of outside services. In a third, providers own the machinery and you rent it. In a fourth, a person can still say stop, though whether that order holds is an open fight. The task is identical in all four. The person who answers for it is not.

This guide is built from a documented source record: four developed futures for law in 2040, and recorded responses from the people who would have to live in them. Use the futures to pressure-test a real decision about people, technology and providers. A way to consider three kinds of change together: the legal practices people rely on, experiments that could change them, and alternatives worth developing. They can coexist rather than arrive in a fixed sequence. connects what to preserve, what to test and what to start building, all at the same time.

Four possible futures

The same legal work.
Different centers of power

One question runs through all four: when your legal work runs on a system somebody built, who can still change its answer, and who pays when nobody can? Each future settles it differently. Read all four before deciding which one your current decisions are building.

Future 01

The company builds the system

The company wires legal checks into the tools employees already use to commit the business. It gains speed and reach, and it becomes answerable for every decision the software clears before a lawyer sees it.

Future 02

Legal directs the network

The legal team keeps the company’s data and buys everything else on its own standards. The price is coordination that never ends: every provider added is a dependency the general counsel now owns.

Step into this future ↗
Future 03

Providers own the infrastructure

No provider asks for your operating knowledge outright. The company hands it over one renewal at a time, because each handover is cheaper than the alternative, until leaving means rebuilding.

Future 04

People claim the final say

A person is named on every consequential decision. Whether that person can read the system, question it and make a stop order stick is the contested part, and this future leaves it contested.

What the futures share / Editorial synthesis

The tools will change. Three questions will not

This work starts from the corporate legal chair, but the questions reach anyone who builds, buys, delivers or relies on legal help.

Ownership decides your room to act

A contract can promise your data back. Whether you can change a service, check a result or act without a provider’s help depends on who runs the systems day to day. Read your renewals for operational control as well as the paper kind.

Human authority needs practical support

A title does not carry authority. A reviewer who cannot see the system’s reasoning, or who lacks standing to stop it, is a signature line. The fourth future asks what would make the review real, and keeps the answer contested.

Access includes the power to challenge

People who cannot afford help, or cannot contest an automated answer, are the stress test of every future here. The consumer and small-business perspective sets the bar: affordable help, fair treatment and a live route of appeal when the system is wrong.

How today connects to tomorrow

Preserve, experiment and build at the same time

You do not have to choose between running today’s legal function and building the next one. Three Horizons holds both jobs, and the experiments between them, in one budget conversation: what must keep working, what you are testing, and what you want the tests to make possible.

H1

Keep what people rely on

Maintain the institutions, expertise and practices that people still need.

H2

Test what could change it

Try different tools or services and examine what they improve, and what they leave unresolved.

H3

Build what comes next

Develop alternatives that could change how legal work is delivered and who can get help.
