A scenario earns its keep as a stress test. Bring a live decision with you and price it in each future: what the company gains, what it must manage, and who ends up holding the decision.

These are scenarios, written as fiction. Backstory dates and company examples are imagined turning points, and no probability attaches to any of the four. The supplier-renewal scene inside each future was written for this guide as an illustration: hypothetical, not a recorded event.

Future 1 of 4 · Scenario

The company builds the system

In this scenario the company stops buying legal outcomes and starts operating them. Risk rules and legal checks live inside the tools employees use to commit the business, so most decisions clear before any lawyer looks. Lawyers keep the exceptions and write the rules. The chief legal officer runs an operation, and answers for it the way an operator does.

Who holds controlThe company builds and controls shared systems for legal work.

What the company gains

Problems get caught where decisions happen, at a speed and coverage no review team can match. The chief legal officer reads the whole week in one Monday digest: thousands of clearances, a handful of exceptions, each already routed to the specialist who should see it. Specialists spend their time on exceptions and new risk instead of routine clearance.

What it must manage

The system works only with deep access to how the company actually operates, and every automated clearance is a decision the company made. When one goes wrong, the question lands on whoever wrote the rules, with no vendor standing in between.

The same task in this future · A supplier renewal

Tuesday, 8:40 a.m. The renewal reaches procurement already cleared: obligations mapped, policy checks passed, escalation rules satisfied, no lawyer involved. The chief legal officer sees it as one line in her weekly digest, and most mornings that reads as victory. This morning the digest flags a clause the system approved under a rule she wrote in 2036, for a market that has since moved on. The renewal itself is fine. The next thousand run on the same rule, and hers is the only name on it.

Future 2 of 4 · Scenario

Legal directs the network

In this scenario the general counsel runs procurement as strategy. Specialist firms and tools stay separate, and the company’s data, standards and purchasing power decide how they connect. Nothing here is exotic. What changes is that coordination stops being overhead and becomes the legal function’s core product, funded and defended like one.

Who holds controlCorporate legal sets the terms for a network of external tools and firms.

What the company gains

Specialist capability on tap, with the company’s knowledge and data still under its own roof. On an ordinary morning that means five services reporting in, each handling one defined piece of the renewal, each replaceable at the end of the quarter if it slips. Providers compete for defined work instead of accumulating it, and the standing threat of replacement keeps their pricing in line.

What it must manage

Keeping the network coherent is a job, and in this future it belongs to the general counsel. His afternoons go to a standards update three vendors read three ways, an integration that broke when one of them shipped early, a hand-off nobody owns until he assigns it. Once a year the same work turns into a line item, and he stands in a budget meeting defending spending that looks, from the outside, like pure friction. If he stopped assigning the hand-offs and defending the budget, the vendors would drift back into separate systems within a few quarters.

The same task in this future · A supplier renewal

The renewal splits on arrival: one service reads the obligations, another runs the market comparison, a firm takes the negotiation posture, each working inside the company’s standards and data permissions. The general counsel spends twenty minutes deciding where the hand-offs happen, which is the job now. It closes clean, and the file shows why: three vendors, one set of standards, his judgment holding them together. His quarterly note reports the network as healthy. The caveat stays in his head: it works because someone spends those twenty minutes on every matter that comes in, and the plan names no successor for that job.

Future 3 of 4 · Scenario

Providers own the infrastructure

In this scenario law firms, legal technology companies and large technology providers pool capital, data and expertise, and companies rent the result. No single contract surrenders anything. The dependence accrues across hundreds of reasonable choices, because using the shared system is cheaper every time, and each use teaches the provider a little more about how your business runs.

Who holds controlAn alliance of firms and technology providers owns the infrastructure.

What the company gains

Speed, scale and capability the company could never build alone, priced as a service instead of a program. Overnight the platform has read the new filings, refreshed the playbooks and benchmarked every open negotiation against a market wider than the company will ever see on its own. The legal team arrives to work that is mostly done, and done well. The learning produced by that work stays with the platform, not with the company.

What it must manage

Exit. When the operating knowledge of your agreements, disputes and compliance sits in a provider’s system, leaving means rebuilding from a standing start. The general counsel learns the real price the day she asks for a migration estimate: the contract data can be exported, but the playbooks, benchmarks and tuned checks the platform built on it are the provider’s property and stay behind. So she manages the dependence instead. She keeps a running list of what the company would need on its first morning off the platform, and she brings it to every renegotiation, because both sides price the deal against the cost of leaving.

The same task in this future · A supplier renewal

The renewal runs through the platform the way everything runs through the platform: fast, standard, benchmarked against ten thousand renewals the company will never see. The general counsel flags one term she wants handled differently. The platform offers three supported options; none is hers. She escalates, and the answer comes back polite and structural: the contract the chief executive signed years ago settled this question before she asked it. The renewal closes on time, on the platform’s terms. She writes the fourth option down anyway.

Future 4 of 4 · Scenario

People own the final say

In this scenario visible failures force the profession to rebuild around people who can supervise machines: new roles, new licenses, a named human on consequential decisions. The scenario’s central claim, that humans stay in charge, is the one it refuses to settle. A signature requirement is easy to legislate. The information, expertise and standing that make a stop order stick are harder to supply.

Who holds controlPeople are meant to retain judgment, escalation and override authority.

What the company gains

Wider access to legal capability, with a designated human route when the stakes are high. A supplier’s owner on the wrong end of an automated answer gets a name to call, and the lawyer who answers holds a license that says she may intervene. Whether the intervention holds is a separate question, and this future leaves it open.

What it must manage

The gap between the title and the power. A reviewer who cannot see the reasoning, lacks the expertise to question it, or has no authority to halt the process is a formality with a salary.

The same task in this future · A supplier renewal

The automated checks clear the renewal, and by every measure the system can read, they are right. The lawyer assigned to it looks past the file to the counterparty: a supplier with eleven employees, an owner she once met on a site visit, and one clause, fine at enterprise scale, that will break a company that size. She invokes her authority and tells the system to stop. The order is logged. This future leaves the outcome open: whether she gets the information to prove her case, and whether her stop order outranks the machine’s record of being right.

Compare all four futures side by side
Owning the technology and making each decision are different forms of control.

A company can own the stack while software makes the calls. That is why the first two futures, where the company controls the service, still raise the fourth future’s question about who can intervene. Expect your organization to recognize pieces of more than one future at once.

Other questions behind the scenarios

These futures also brush against questions the three axes cannot hold: whether the technology can be understood and trusted, which work lawyers keep, how careers form, how much work machines perform, and how public reaction or regulation could redraw the picture. Connected systems can still be opaque. Human participation can vary task by task inside one future.
