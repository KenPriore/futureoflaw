Choices for today / Four perspectives

Different roles. Different decisions to make

Read these four sets of proposals as a map of your counterparties. In-house teams decide what to own. Firms decide what to sell. Technology providers decide what access to demand. Consumers and small businesses decide when to stop trusting the process. Your strategy has to meet all four.

Each seat weighed all four futures. What follows are recorded proposals, offered here as starting points for your own discussion. They are not a shared plan, and none is a record of action taken.

How the four seats used the futures
Four seats, the same four futures.

The people who built the scenarios were not the people who responded to them, and each seat read all four worlds through its own stakes, so one future can produce opposite priorities for a company, a provider and a person seeking help. The responses sort proposals three ways: expected to hold up across futures, dependent on conditions, and aimed at keeping the ability to adapt. Those labels record judgment calls, not proven results.

The sharpest move here sorts legal roles by the work involved, the judgment required and the trust placed in the person, before any outsourcing decision. A sourcing strategy that starts from cost gives judgment away by accident. One that starts from this map gives it away only on purpose.

One concrete proposal / From the in-house record
“Build an override switch in case of failure.”

The in-house record offers this as insurance against failure. Treat it as a test, not a standard: an override exists only if a specific person has the information to see the failure, the skill to judge it and the authority to make the stop hold. Nothing in the record says that standard was agreed, and nothing says one was built.

Decisions still open / Guide interpretation

Proposals need owners, resources and a test of success

The record ends where your work starts: with proposals and no delivery plan. Three questions carry the discussion into your organization.

Who would take responsibility?

A proposal needs someone to lead it, resources to carry it out, and a way to assess whether it helps.

Could a person intervene effectively?

A right to challenge an automated decision depends on usable information, relevant expertise and authority to act.

Whose experience needs to be tested?

No set of proposals written about consumers and small businesses can stand in for them. Their direct participation is how you test the promised benefits and find the needs the proposals missed.
