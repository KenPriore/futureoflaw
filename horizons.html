Change does not arrive all at once

Keep today’s system working
Make room for what comes next

Leaders usually get the future presented as a fork: defend the current model or bet on the new one. A framework for discussing change through three overlapping patterns: what works today, experiments in transition, and a different future beginning to take shape. A horizon describes a pattern, rather than a deadline. replaces the fork with three concurrent jobs: keep essential legal services dependable, run experiments that teach you something specific, and develop the alternative you actually want. All three belong in the same plan and the same budget.

How to use the two frameworks

Three horizons, four possible futures Different purposes

The four scenarios explore how legal work could be organized. The three horizons help us think about changing it. H1, H2 and H3 can all be present today. They are not three deadlines, and H3 does not predict which scenario will happen.

Conceptual diagram. The curves show changing patterns, not measured data, probabilities or a timetable.

H1 / What people rely on today

Keep essential legal work dependable

Keeping the current system dependable is a decision, staffed and funded, and it earns the right to experiment elsewhere. Picture the matter that lands the week your most experienced contracts lawyer leaves: it still gets reviewed, someone still owns the exception, and the business never feels the gap. This horizon asks what must keep working while everything else moves, and where cost, delay and access already fail the people who depend on it.

What must keep working while we make changes?

H2 / What we can test

Use experiments to learn what could change

A pilot that cannot change a decision is theater. Picture the meeting where one ends instead: a result on the table, a date that forced it there, and one person who has to say what changes now. This horizon holds each experiment to a finding: name what it should teach you, by when, and what you would do differently if the result surprises you. Some experiments improve today’s approach; the interesting ones make a different approach possible.

What would this experiment help us learn, and what might it change?

H3 / What we want to make possible

Develop a different way to deliver legal help

Someone has to fund the early version of the system you would rather work in. Picture the line in next year’s budget with no client and no deadline behind it, only the future it is meant to open. This horizon names that future and asks what you could start developing now: new professional roles, services built on shared technology, wider access to help, different grounds for trust. If you skip it, you inherit whichever future someone else paid for.

What future are we trying to support, and what could we start developing now?

Illustration / One decision, three horizons

Apply all three to a supplier renewal

Take the supplier renewal this guide asks you to hold in mind and put it to work: the same task, sorted three ways inside one plan and one budget. These examples show how to use the framework. They were written for this guide, separate from the recorded proposals.

H1

Keep renewals dependable

The renewal that arrives this quarter still gets reviewed, approved and owned. Whatever else the plan changes, the business keeps making agreements, and a named person keeps answering for the exceptions.

H2

Test a defined change

Run one type of renewal through automated checks beside the existing review. Set the comparison date before the pilot starts, and name in advance who acts on whatever the results show.

H3

Explore a different service

Start sketching the service where the renewal never disappears between signatures: obligations tracked through the agreement’s life, lawyers holding the judgment calls, the exceptions and the ways problems get resolved.

The raw material

Seventeen possibilities to explore

Before the four futures existed, an earlier stage of the record produced seventeen sketches of law in 2040. They helped set the questions the futures answer. Some conflict with one another, which is the point: they map the space of possibility, not today’s market or an agreed plan.

Explore the 17 earlier visions

Technology and legal services

Legal data is easier to share

In this vision, shared data underpins how legal systems operate. Legal data standards are established, and commercial incentives support contributing to and accessing shared datasets. Trust in AI systems is closely tied to the quality and provenance of the data they are built on.

Data shapes decisions and access to legal help

In this vision, large-scale datasets shape precedent, prediction, and outcomes. Abundant data makes legal capability more widely accessible, but data remains unevenly available, contributing to opacity in parts of the system.

Shared digital records support trust

This vision proposes Using a shared digital ledger to record and verify transactions. This vision imagines such records taking on functions now performed by institutions that verify information or transactions; nothing here establishes that this future will occur. shared digital records for verification, agreements and enforcement. Those records would perform some functions now handled by institutions that verify information or transactions.

Reliable AI supports more automated legal work

In this vision, AI reliability supports wider automation of legal reasoning and decision making. Rules are codified into predictive and executable systems with machine judgment at greater speed and consistency.

Machines handle more routine legal reasoning

In this vision, machines interpret rules and carry out routine legal work. People remain important for complex, disputed, strategic or high-consequence decisions.

Legal services run through repeatable processes

In this vision, legal services are delivered through integrated systems as well as individuals. As production becomes systematized, differentiation shifts toward strategic judgment, negotiation, creativity, and trusted relationships.

Legal help becomes an everyday service

In this vision, legal services are more accessible, lower cost, and embedded across business and society. Law functions more like infrastructure that enables systems and transactions to operate.

People and legal careers

Clients choose judgment and context

In this vision, clients choose providers for judgment, understanding of their circumstances and the way advice fits their needs. Size and staffing matter less as legal capability becomes more widely available.

Lawyers take on different roles

In this vision, outcomes vary: lawyers as strategic advisers and system designers; or a fragmented profession of specialized experts; or new hybrid roles blurring law, technology, and business.

Routes into legal careers change

In this vision, training and apprenticeship are reshaped, but not uniformly. Erosion of entry-level work can create bottlenecks; new alternative credentials and fluid routes also emerge.

People’s role in automated work is deliberately designed

In this vision, services deliberately choose where people take part: for empathy, context, ethical judgment, trust or oversight. Other processes involve people less often, raising different questions about responsibility.

Institutions and accountability

Disputes are resolved in more settings

In this vision, AI-supported judges, virtual environments, and community-based processes handle a significant share of disputes; physical courts remain important in some contexts.

Different routes to justice produce different outcomes

In this vision, justice varies depending on where and how disputes are resolved, with variation in access, speed, cost, and fairness; bias can be embedded in design or data.

Rules are set by a wider range of organizations

In this vision, regulation is distributed across multiple actors, adaptive in form, and contested. State-led frameworks sit alongside platform, network, and industry-group rules.

Compliance checks are built into everyday work

In this vision, law is built into products, workflows, and infrastructure. Compliance is continuous and automated; legal requirements are translated into code.

Technology handles more dispute resolution

In this vision, a large share of disputes is handled through technology-enabled processes; AI-driven arbitration and automated resolution routes are widely used.

Agreements respond to events and help prevent disputes

In this vision, legal systems prevent disputes as well as resolve them. Agreements are dynamic and data-driven; contracts function as structured data that helps govern behavior.
